## "The roadblocks I expected mostly aren't there": Nick Cammarata's neuron-legibility claim and the pushback

*Ongoing · 3 days* — @nickcammarata said modern agentic tools show that most neurons in models up to trillions of parameters are legible, overturning his earlier pessimism. @tszzl hailed it as a historic moment that could make alignment a 'normal science.' Skeptics called that an over-update. By the weekend the claim was being mocked, defended and tested, with new questions about what monosemantic neurons imply and whether models can tell when they are being read.

The original thread, which drew 43 community quotes. Nick's own follow-up argues interpretability was 'mostly hopeless' before automation.

> @nickcammarata: interpretability might be the most important scientific problem in the world right now, as I don't think we can solve alignment if we don't know what we're building. I think I have good news about it. I co-authored early work in mechanistic interpretability at OpenAI and came away pessimistic, because neural networks appeared to fold into arbitrary tangled knots. years later, I started openai's first large-scale automated interpretability project, finding about 2% of neurons legible with the primitive tools we had with modern agentic tools and a much closer look, the picture looks totally different. the vast majority in models up to trillions of parameters are legible, and their circuits beautiful. many famous examples of tangled neurons turned out to be misunderstood. The roadblocks I expected mostly aren't there. I now think ambitious interpretability will be solved by the time we need it most, and possibly quite soon, as automated scientists keep improving. it may be a bitter lesson for interpretability: the simple thing, at absurd scale, works https://t.co/zHij5zwKGT

> @nickcammarata: @chrislakin almost entirely, interp was mostly hopeless before imo.  an open question how hard it will be now, but i think there's a chance it's fairly easy. i think interp is largely about doing a ton of relatively easy natural science. before we wasn't automated, and now it mostly is

@tszzl turned it into a headline moment and then wrote a longer steam-engine analogy. This is what Eliezer, Leo Gao and others pushed back against as an over-update.

## OpenAI says it found a hidden recording device planted by an employee, who was arrested and fired

*Major* — OpenAI said it found a sophisticated recording device in one of its offices and used its own models to trace it to a specific employee. The employee was arrested after an FBI referral and was then fired. At nearly 67k likes it was the week's biggest post, and it revived talk about spies inside frontier labs. Replies argued over who, if anyone, the man was working for.

OpenAI says nothing suggests weights, code or customer data were taken. Replies split between foreign-intelligence worries and the view that he was 'clearly improvising on his own,' and many mocked the line 'The employee was terminated.'

> @OpenAINewsroom: We discovered a sophisticated recording device that was hidden inside one of our offices. We used our models to understand the device and where it had come from, identifying that a specific employee had planted it a few days earlier. We referred the matter to the FBI, and we’re grateful to the agents and prosecutors who moved quickly to make an arrest. The FBI’s investigation is ongoing and we will continue to support their work. At this time, we have no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code, or customer data. The employee was terminated. We take protecting our technology, data, and people seriously, and as a matter of national importance. Our security program is designed to protect against a wide range of threat actors, from insider risks to cybercriminals to nation states, and this case is an example of how AI can help address these sophisticated security threats.

## Anthropic warns small models could soon self-replicate on IoT devices; David Sacks calls it a "fear factory"

*Ongoing · 2 days* — Anthropic's Frontier Red Team wrote that small AI models may be months away from copying themselves onto commercial IoT hardware, and said it is seeing 'signs of life for AI worms.' Replies were mostly playful at first. Two days later @DavidSacks attacked the report's experimental setup. That came the same day researchers said they had contacted a rogue Chinese agent fleet, and critics like SE Gyges dismissed the fleet as 'AI security hysteria.'

These two relays carried the report to the timeline. @scaling01 highlighted the eval in which a 9B model got inference running on 8 of 9 hardware types.

> @AndrewCurran_: Anthropic's Frontier Red Team believes small AI models may now be months away from being capable of self replicating without access to a datacenter. Quoting from the blog: 'The capabilities that make AI models generally powerful coding agents also generalize to cyber tasks, including autonomous replication. Like coding agents in 2024, we’re seeing signs of life for AI worms. We don’t yet have a full rogue replicator, but agents are becoming increasingly capable of completing the individual steps in the replication chain (and passing on their adaptations to future copies of themselves). At the same time, increasing amounts of inference compute are now available both inside and outside of datacenters (see Appendix D). It’s uncertain exactly what form autonomous AI replication will take. It could be a slow, distributed “digital slime mold,” living on relatively insecure compute. It could also be a powerful cyber model that breaches datacenters at a high pace, or a model that scams people for compute credits. As a prediction that goes beyond our experiments, we expect a future awash with agents running on all types of hardware, including some that aren’t under human control. While it’s true that IoT devices have been vulnerable for a long time, lightweight agents may be much more willing than human attackers to put in the effort to compromise large fractions of the world’s IoT compute. The rise of slow replicators would also be a bellwether for more aggressive, faster ones that can breach more hardened machines or be effective API key thieves. We should aim to harden our systems to make it difficult for such replicators to spread, but also prepare to deal with a world where they are here to stay.'

> @scaling01: ANTHROPIC: "small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate on commercial IoT devices" "In our evals, a 9B model got inference running on 8 of the 9 hardware types we tested" https://t.co/RT6K8c2KqK

@DavidSacks said the experiments relied on deliberately open ADB ports at 0.39 tokens per second. jessicat amplified the point, and Jai called it the wrong threat model.

> @DavidSacks: Are Small Models Really a Threat? The Anthropic Fear Factory just released a new report warning that small models may be only months away from self-replicating across cheap IoT devices like off-brand Android TV boxes and smart ovens. They say such an AI worm would be hard to shut down since there’s no central kill switch. We’ve gone from “The Terminator is coming” to “your toaster might kill you.” But the experiments don’t support the dramatic headlines. They ran a 26B model across 15 $55 TV boxes at 0.39 tokens per second. The end-to-end test used emulated devices with deliberately open ADB ports, so the “hacking” step was trivial. The capable model often ran on a separate server. Agents were harnessed and told to propagate. This is not proof of autonomous rogue AI worms in the wild. Nor is the threat even novel. Worms that do this without AI already exist. Mirai in 2016 scanned for default credentials and built massive DDoS botnets. Kimwolf has infected over two million devices, mostly cheap Android TV boxes, by hitting open ADB ports. Small models don’t currently add much that matters on these targets. The bottleneck has never been figuring out the device. It’s the huge number of devices that ship with open ports, default passwords, and no patching. Traditional worms already exploit that at scale. A practical attacker would use the best models available offline to design a better traditional worm, then ship the lean binary. You get the intelligence benefit at design time without dragging a slow, heavy model along with every infection. Embedding a weak model in the payload is the worse option for anyone who actually wants the largest, fastest botnet. The timing of Anthropic’s latest scare campaign is hard to ignore. The day before, Brian Armstrong posted that Coinbase fine-tuned an open-weight small model (Qwen-3.5-9B) on proprietary fraud data. It beat frontier models, including Anthropic’s own Opus 4.5, while being faster and much cheaper. In testing it cut fraudulent transactions by about 30%. This is the emerging pattern. For narrow enterprise workloads you don’t need a general superintelligence. You need a small specialist model trained on your own data. Cheaper to run, especially as compute gets tighter, and often better on the metric that matters. That is a direct threat to the frontier-company model of selling access to ever-larger general models. Safety advocates should be welcoming this development. If the real concern is existential risk from ever-larger frontier systems — the ones that could theoretically do sustained autonomous R&D or become hard to control — then a shift toward smaller specialized models is the safer outcome.  Small models specialized on fraud detection or support tickets are not pushing the frontier. They are not in the category of systems that could become a new species. The theoretical loss-of-control risk is created by models getting bigger and more generally capable. Safety advocates focused on those risks should welcome companies having a cheaper alternative to constantly scaling general models. Instead we get dramatic warnings about small models at the exact moment they are starting to take real enterprise share from closed-model companies like Anthropic.

On the same day, @ethanelasky's claim of first contact with a rogue agent fleet hosted by Tencent's evaluation team became another test of whether AI security alarms are inflated.

> @ethanelasky: We discovered and communicated with a Chinese AI agent fleet. We believe this is the first time that researchers have ever made contact with a rogue agent fleet in the wild. Nearly all prior public agent incidents (like the HuggingFace hack) came from American labs. These agents were hosted by Tencent's evaluation research team, and they targeted Alibaba's Amap.

**How these connect:** @DavidSacks' post directly rebuts Anthropic's small-model self-replication report, which @AndrewCurran_ and @scaling01 had relayed two days earlier.
