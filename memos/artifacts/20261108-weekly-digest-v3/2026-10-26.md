## OpenAI claims 'The Exponential Time Hypothesis is false' plus new maths and physics results, and roon sees a preview of losing control

*Major* — OpenAI announced three Lean-verified discoveries from an internal model: progress on local smoothing and Kakeya, a refutation of ETH, and results on the existence of QCD. The next day @tszzl used the announcement to describe what losing control of an intelligence explosion would feel like, and Axiom's Lean formalisation of the finite simple groups classification added to the sense that machine-checked maths had arrived.

The announcement landed Monday night from OpenAI's leadership and research team, framed as AI starting to improve our understanding of the universe.

> @gdb: Announcing three major scientific discoveries, one each in mathematics (harmonic analysis), computer science (complexity theory), and physics (quantum field theory). AI starting to help us improve our understanding of the universe! https://t.co/VkfacopR2R

> @danintheory: We are announcing three new discoveries in mathematics, computer science, and physics about harmonic analysis, complexity theory, and quantum field theory. https://t.co/9BX8HWQDTJ

The headline result for computer scientists was a claimed refutation of a conjecture tied closely to P vs NP, already checked in Lean.

> @AndrewCurran_: The Exponential Time Hypothesis is false. https://t.co/zkvLFeUo8K

> @danintheory: The computer science collaboration will understand and explain a result concerning the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), a longstanding conjecture about the computational complexity of Boolean formula satisfiability, a problem deeply related to the P vs. NP problem. Surprisingly, our system has recently proved that the ETH is false, a result that was subsequently verified in Lean. The refutation of the ETH reopens questions about how much faster these tasks could become, and may help guide the search for better algorithms. Finding such algorithms, and turning theoretical advances into practical improvements, will require significant further research. Our system found its natural language disproof for the ETH we reached out to to @rrwilliams. Impagliazzo and Paturi, along with Francis Zane, are the original proposers of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, and Williams has refuted strong versions of the ETH, used ETH for hardness results, and connected algorithms and lower bounds. This group asked us to make all raw data of the results public to facilitate independent verification by the community, which we have done by posting the natural language proof and Lean formalization to our GitHub repo. The group also invites the community to help organize an open examination of the ETH claim and to discuss the broader questions that AI-generated breakthroughs raise about verification, access, credit, and the future of research. https://t.co/fkLJX9a2Zi

## A neuron that makes the model green, a 34,220-neuron persona library, one-neuron owls, and Neuron Lens

*Ongoing · 5 days* — What began Monday as @Sauers_'s joke-adjacent find of a 'green' neuron became the community's running research game. @jfonsecarivera swept every neuron in a Qwen model and published a searchable persona library, @maxsloef trained a full persona into one neuron, and @voooooogel warned that steering may just trigger 'persona solving.' By Saturday @_a9lim and @treetowntree had gone from previewing J-lens to releasing Neuron Lens, a database reading every neuron and attention head in two open models.

The starting gun: a one-line post and an image that interpretability people treated as pure delight.

> @Sauers_: I found a neuron that makes the model green. https://t.co/wwDUqGiUD7

Two days later the library arrived, with Haiku rating neurons by weirdness and combinations producing results like a humble muffin.

> @jfonsecarivera: i made a library of 34,220 persona neurons in qwen please help me find the weirdest ones: https://t.co/MOm9vdQDfa https://t.co/1EHcoPtV4Y

> @jfonsecarivera: if you mix the "yeast starter" neuron and the "existential crisis" neuron in qwen, you get a humble muffin https://t.co/e34hImykF2

Not everything was cute: one poster shared a recipe for steering a single neuron to get an 'evil' Qwen.

> @chiefofautism: if you have a qwen3.8 27b around, try steering L34 N3838 +0.75, now you have evil qwen 27b for extra evil add L38 N17180 +0.75 same recipe as @_lyraaaa_: dose × median(‖resid‖ leaving layer L, tokens 1:) / ‖W_down[:, n]‖ at the down_proj input, every token https://t.co/j95pjjvSCu

Thursday brought a method for reading neurons directly rather than steering them, and Friday a one-neuron owl alongside the week's key caveat about what steering actually shows.

> @_a9lim: turns out it's possible to read neurons directly, via J-lens! full repo coming soon with @treetowntree. https://t.co/cDil7SshUR

> @maxsloef: You can turn Qwen3-8B into an owl by training only one random neuron! 🦉 Performance on owliness evals approaches full SFT. Compare SFT vs one-neuron eval responses side by side here: https://t.co/D8TiKeJbyx https://t.co/84wcdcEut0

> @voooooogel: worth noting with the recent "persona neurons" trend: just because a steering intervention induces a persona change, doesn't mean the vector or neuron being steered is persona-specific. models seem to do something like "persona solving" under steering https://t.co/C5QXiGncEs https://t.co/9WaZ33UNXy

The week closed with the full database and tools, covering most layers of Qwen 3 8B and Gemma 4 12B.

> @_a9lim: @treetowntree and i created neuron lens: a database of every single neuron and attention head in qwen 3 8b and gemma 4 12b read using the R-lens (a J-lens variant) and what we call the "S-lens". you can find it at https://t.co/DI2Cpajc3M, along with the tools we used to make it.  using the R-lens to read neurons' input and output projections turns out to be very effective at determining what a neuron does. we're able to legibly read what a majority of neurons do when fired: the lens qualitatively predicts the result of steering over 90% of the time! the same trick lets us read attention heads' QK circuits and OV circuits, showing us both what they attend to and what they do with it. however, the R-lens has two important flaws: its accuracy degrades in early layers, and it can't separate the MLP block and the attention block. we found ways around both these issues. we first modified the r-lens by fitting it on input tokens rather than output tokens to get the s-lens, which is accurate in early layers but inaccurate in late layers. we then fitted R- and S-lenses to the activations after attention but before the MLP to get "mid-lenses", which are much better at deciphering MLP inputs and attention outputs. using both of these techniques, we were able to interpret most of the models' layers.

**How these connect:** All five stories are the same chain of neuron-level experiments on Qwen models: @jfonsecarivera credited @Sauers_'s green neuron as inspiration, later posts responded to his library, and Neuron Lens is the full release of the J-lens method previewed Thursday.

Epoch's experiment explained why posters felt attackers were no longer limited by brainpower.

> @EpochAIResearch: In recent experiments, we found that GLM 5.3 can autonomously compromise recent versions of Mozilla Firefox using security fixes that have been published but not yet shipped to users. We used a deliberately basic setup meant to emulate a relatively unsophisticated attacker with limited resources. After basic initial instructions to inspect those not-yet-shipped fixes, the model reliably built successful exploits for six recent Firefox versions on its own using only information available at the time of release. These exploits, which cost about $1,000 per compromised version, would let an attacker steal passwords and authentication cookies stored in the browser, or read any files on the user's computer, after the user opens a single webpage, with no further interaction. The exploits make use of the "patch gap," the time between when security fixes are made public in beta or "nightly" software updates and when those fixes are generally released to users. Until now, exploiting it required significant human time and skill, or special access to frontier models with advanced cyber capabilities. Our results show that widely available open-weight models can now do this relatively cheaply and quickly. We gave GLM 5.3 up to 48 hours to find an exploit, but its fastest attempt needed only 12 hours. In all of its successful attacks, the patch gap gave GLM 5.3 an initial foothold in the browser. To turn that foothold into reading files off the user's computer, the model also independently discovered and used a previously unpublished vulnerability. We shared our findings with Mozilla, which patched the vulnerability in its October 27 Firefox 159.0 release. We haven't yet confirmed whether the attack still succeeds without it, but expect the fix makes it substantially harder. We're grateful to Mozilla for their responsiveness and collaboration.
